About the Novel

The Reefs of Earth (Berkley Medallion, 1968) follows the Dulanty family, Puca (alien fairy-folk) who have been living on Earth for ten years. When tragedy strikes, the six surviving children (or seven, if you count Bad John) decide upon a simple solution to their problems: kill everyone else on Earth.

What follows is a comic fairy tale set in Lost Haven, Oklahoma, blending Irish folklore, Catholic theology, frontier violence, and Lafferty's linguistic exuberance.

The Chapter Poem

Lafferty structures the novel's sixteen chapter titles as a single Bagarthach-style poem, a prophecy of violence and transformation.

Analysis

The sixteen titles can be read as a Bagarthach-like prophecy. They move from the children’s murderous purpose through their difficulty fitting Earth and their affinity with the dead to a battle whose announced alternatives are an altered world or a dead Dulanty.

Structural Notes

The poem consists of four ABAB quatrains: Land/Band and Roastie/Ghostie, for example, form the first alternating set. Its last two lines do not form a rhyming couplet. The closing line names the two possible outcomes.

The Heroic Chantey

Chapter 14's title, "Fit Subject For Heroic Chantey," promises that Henry's death will be commemorated in song. The chapter delivers, with Latin and English verse fragments appearing as section headers, culminating in the epitaph: "Nil de Monstris Nisi Bonum" ("Nothing about monsters except good").

Meter and Movement

The contents-page titles use four ABAB quatrains with a predominantly iambic four-beat movement. The second and fourth lines of each stanza have feminine endings: roastie/ghostie, quit it/fit it, lindens/Indians, chantey/Dulanty. The movement is jaunty, like a nursery rhyme. The Heroic Chantey later shifts toward a falling rhythm; the two poems differ in meter.

Synopsis

The children’s campaign and the adults’ persecution cross through an axe, a set of hired captors, and a final hunt. All page references are to the 1968 Berkley text.

Two Families in Lost Haven

Henry and Witchy Dulanty are parents of Elizabeth, Charles, and Helen. Frank and Veronica are parents of Peter, Dorothy, John, and the already dead Bad John. The two brothers married two sisters. Sent to Earth more than ten years earlier, the adults have failed to establish accord with its people. Their six-week residence in Lost Haven ends in catastrophe.

The children’s customary ghost stories open the book. Chapter 2 then gives the family’s arrival and decline: Helen curses Crocker, Peter curses Stutgard, and the adults suffer Earth Allergy. Veronica dies two days before Henry’s arrest. Witchy is confined, and Frank’s grief and drinking give way to an illness that can resemble drunkenness even when he is sober.

The Axe and the Raft

The children decide to kill everyone else, though Elizabeth and Charles initially favour a shorter list. Their attempt to burn the adults with a Bagarthach is countered. John reaches Stutgard’s room to murder him and finds Crocker has already done it. John takes Crocker’s bloody axe; Elizabeth later hides it. Henry is arrested for the murder.

From Fulbert, held in the flimsy Lost Haven jail, the children obtain the Île de France. They attack the Stinkers, Stutgard Landing, and Fiddler’s Landing, then encounter Phoebe Jane’s hospitality and raid the Fabers. Gunfire, attempted drownings, and arson make their intent real, though their targets escape.

Parallel Struggles

Frank evades a lynch mob and humiliates Crocker with its noose. Witchy recites her Devil verses in the institution at Vinita. The children find a home among the dead of Misu Mound, where the history of the burials is disputed; Peter suffers a disturbing passion of Earth Sickness. Henry’s confession overwhelms an old priest, whose death a month later is attributed to it by the narrator.

Crocker hires Baxter Bushyhead, Little Sad Sam Jones, and Harold Harvestman to capture the children. Charles marches them into the woods and returns with ears. The narrator’s confirmation of a silencer, together with Dorothy’s later completed accusation, is strong evidence of killings offstage. Pandemonium John brings news from the parents and leads a great concert, mixing delight, cruelty, and musical invention.

Marshall’s interview with Henry exposes both racial hatred and the fraud supporting Lost Haven: bogus relief checks and crop claims, with Marshall expecting to inherit Stutgard’s business. The lawyer’s visit brings out the axe evidence, but Henry mistakes approaching help for danger and escapes at 8:55 p.m. in Chapter 12. Marshall turns the escape into a murderous public spectacle.

The Hunt and the Bog

John Loose Screw and Sammy Bluefield help Henry. After a roadblock and crash, Henry reaches Thomas Butterfield’s farm. Butterfield phones the authorities despite Henry’s warnings. Marshall shoots Henry in the shoulder from behind, then in the groin and gut; Crocker fires the fatal shot into his face. Marshall kills Butterfield to remove the witness.

Crocker hunts Bad John through the bogs as his dogs desert him in groups. Frank catches him with the noose. Frank intends afterward to urge the children toward gentleness, but Marshall strikes him from behind before he can speak to them. The rope slips and Crocker sinks. Marshall rolls Frank into the bog. Charles runs up firing; Phoebe Jane’s hidden shot kills Marshall. Charles believes he did it and rolls Marshall’s body in.

Names and Departure

That afternoon the children intuit their Puca names. Bad John has already been absent for hours. After they cast off, Witchy joins them through Phoebe’s aid and explains that Bad John has gone with Frank. Witchy revises the extermination plan toward control and alteration, still permitting killing and losing her thought about responsibility. She releases the Dragons’ Seed, continues through her last joke about dying, and receives Elizabeth’s answer. Six young pairs of eyes shine beside the older pair fading in death.

Six little pair of goblin eyes glowing green in the dark; and a seventh older pair nictitated by death slumber to a fainter green. And opposed to them, only the defenseless World!
— Narrator

Chapter 1

To Slay the Folks and Cleanse the Land

The Dulanty children tell ghost stories in the Big Shanty, exorcising the bleakness of Earth through whistling in the dark.

Summary

The novel opens with an introduction to the Dulanty family: Puca aliens living in the Big Shanty in Lost Haven, Oklahoma. The six children (or seven, if you count Bad John) gather at night to tell ghost stories, their way of defying the "meanest world in all the universes."

The six tales vary in their dealings with death. Elizabeth’s concerns Albert and his unnamed wife, whose verse releases a murderous dog from a picture. Charles tells of a hotel man found dead; Helen of the still-whittling Silly Jimmy; Peter of Alice, Clarissa, and the guard from Hell; Dorothy of a train passing through the shanty; and John of William’s terrible return to Polly.

Bad John, the ghost child, cannot participate in the ritual: "he could only tell people stories, and nobody cares about them." He has no bed in the loft where the other children sleep. When the lights go out, nobody knows where he goes. He "couldn't whistle" in the dark.

Key Events

  • Introduction of all seven children and their relationships
  • Establishment of the Aorach story form
  • First description of the Puca appearance (goblin vs. human-looking)
  • Introduction of the green glowing eyes motif
  • Establishment of Bad John's nature as a ghost child

Elizabeth's Story: The Haunted Picture

A Puca couple buys a picture of a man. The wife notices the eyes change from green to brown, the hair from red to black. Then she notices a dog in the picture that wasn't there before. She makes a careless Bagarthach verse hoping the dog bites her husband, and it does, killing him.

You'll find that picture's got a dog!

— The wife in Elizabeth's story

Moral: "Earth things are so dumb that they don't know when to obey a Bagarthach and when not to."

Charles's Story: The Country Hotel

Charles’s traveling salesman finds a terrifying instability of identities at a country hotel. The tale identifies the corpse as the hotel man, with his throat cut. The building vanishes, and the doorknob the traveler carries becomes a rock.

Peter's Story: Alice and Clarissa

Alice was sent to Hell for Clarissa’s theft. Returning to frighten her sister, she loses her ghost sheet to Clarissa. The guard mistakes Clarissa for Alice and carries Clarissa away; Alice stays on Earth.

Dorothy's Story: The Ghost Train

Dorothy tells of the dead lantern-bearer and the train that killed him. The train takes the old track beneath the shanty. She says she made a sign: it passed through without breaking the house up.

§
John's Story: William and Polly

A Puca boy named William marries an Earth girl named Polly. They promise that whoever dies first will return to tell the other what death is like. William dies; Polly remarries a man named Tom. On their wedding night, someone comes to the door three times. Tom relays the messages: "He said to tell you that it sure is cold." Polly says it must be the coal man. "He said to tell you that it sure is dark." The kerosene man. "He said to tell you that it sure is lonesome." Polly tells Tom to send him away: "I don't care any more how it is there."

A great wind kills Tom, freezes Polly stiff, blows away the house, opens a hole, and buries Polly. William’s messages tell Polly what death is like: cold, dark, lonesome.

Puca Lore Introduced

  • Aorach: a Puca story art, including these ghost tales but also Veronica’s story of the sisters’ births.
  • Bagarthach verse: Rhymed verses with magical power; Earth things "are so dumb that they don't know when to obey a Bagarthach and when not to"
  • Puca appearance: Some look human, others are "potato-faced goblins, like real Pucas." Elizabeth, Charles, and Helen can pass as Earth children; Peter, Dorothy, John, and Bad John have the true goblin look. Witchy "said that they had planned it that way, that some of the children should look like children of this place, and some of them should look like the children back home."
  • Green eyes: "Look at them straight, and you'd hardly notice a thing. But look at them sideways... they'd scare the liver out of you." Eyes that "glowed like green coals."

The Irish Words Behind the Arts

The Puca name recalls Irish púca, a fairy or goblin; Peter’s Ifreann is the Irish word for hell. The threatening verse belongs to a wider tradition of dangerous Irish rhymers: Rosalind’s joke about having been an Irish rat in As You Like It 3.2 presupposes the belief that rats could be rhymed to death. Rosalind’s joke is a literary parallel for the Bagarthach.

Sources: Shakespeare, As You Like It 3.2.

Chapter 2

And Leave the World a Reeking Roastie

The Dulantys arrive in Lost Haven, where hostility greets them, and Helen kills a man with a verse before he knows he's dead.

Summary

This chapter provides the backstory: how the Dulanty families came to Lost Haven six weeks before the main action. They traveled in two station wagons and two trucks: Veronica drove one wagon with all the children, Witchy drove the other "loaded with everything in the world," Henry drove the sideboard truck with the furniture, and Frank drove the van carrying "the tools and shops of the several professions of the two brothers."

They stopped for lunch in what appeared to be a weed patch. A hostile man appeared, barking at them: "We won't have our town dirtied up by strangers. You're the strangest clutch I ever saw." Henry demanded: "Where's any town?" The man replied: "You're standing in the middle of Lost Haven", the town so overgrown it was invisible. This was Crocker. Helen killed him with a Bagarthach verse, though it would take weeks for him to realize he was dead.

Half an hour later, a bigger man arrived: Coalfactor Stutgard, owner of the town. "I don't know what you are," he said, "but you don't belong anywhere on God's green Earth." Peter whispered to the children, "Let me kill this one," and growled his own killing verse. A furtive local then told them Stutgard lied: one house wasn't his: the Big Shanty, owned by an Indian lady in Catoosa.

The chapter also reveals the Puca mission: sent to Earth as "picked young couples" to achieve accord with humanity, or failing that, to "do something about the planet." Ten years in, they have achieved neither.

Veronica, "the real center of the two families," mother to some children and aunt to the rest, dies suddenly of Earth Allergy. Frank takes her death "all wrong," his merriment at the wake forced, his Bagarthach verses falling short. Watching their children play, Henry observes: "They can't really adapt to Earth, I see it now. The question then becomes: Can the Earth adapt to them?" The chapter ends with Henry raising his head, listening "as to the sounding of a horn off Earth."

Puca Lore

  • Earth Allergy: Fatal to adult Puca; attacks liver and kidneys (seat of good humor); taints one with the "Earth outlook"
  • The mission: achieve accord with Earth and consider whether something could be done with the planet.
  • The Four Worlds: Earth belongs to a group the novel does not fully identify; Mercator, Astrobe, and Camiroi appear elsewhere.
  • Indian Affinity: Puca were told they'd be "in rapport with the Indian population" but found them a "vanishing minority"; their information was "centuries out of date"
  • The Hive Ghost: Frank’s account of humanity’s alarming communal instinct. His claim that Earth people act without thinking is a Puca judgment.

The Aorach of Veronica's Conception

Veronica tells her characteristic Aorach story explaining how she and Witchy were "twins" born eleven months apart, conceived the same night but by separate acts, when their mother briefly went to bed at one minute before 4 a.m. (when Papa had to light the furnace at the brickyard). Pilgrims and Predicted Deaths

The opening “pilgrims on Earth” recalls Hebrews 11:13 and 1 Peter 2:11, where human life is understood as a pilgrimage. The Dulantys give that language an extraterrestrial sense. Peter’s “jackals bearing axes” adapts the warning about Greeks bearing gifts associated with Virgil’s Laocoon. Crocker and his axe will make the adapted warning literal. Helen’s “be belled” recalls Aesop’s mice and their plan to bell the cat; Frank later brings the noose.

Sources: Virgil, Aeneid 2.49; Aesop, “Belling the Cat”.

Chapter 3

High Purpose of the Gallant Band

The children conceive their great plan: kill everyone in the world except the six of them (or seven, if you count Bad John).

Summary

The chapter opens with the children's logical conclusion: "If there were only six persons in the world (or seven, if you count Bad John), the Earth would be a much better place." Their plan is simple: kill everyone. Elizabeth dissents, willing to kill only specific targets: "the first man to bark at them" (Crocker), Mrs. Cowper with "eyes like a little pig," Mr. Cranker with "hairs in his ears." Charles adds the Bacons, Lanyards, Kirbys, Fabers, plus Elwood Elgin, Rex Remagen, Barney Bottleby, and Sheriff Train, but thinks "that was almost enough." Helen overrules them: "The whole thing becomes silly unless you kill everybody." She hints that Elizabeth, at nine, might be "borderline" herself.

Dorothy sings a Bagarthach verse to burn down the Big Shanty with the adults inside. The children spend the afternoon swimming: Elizabeth the strongest swimmer, Helen the fastest and slipperiest, Charles diving from the very tops of the tall sycamores. While drying on branches, four tell parabolic stories about recent traumas: Elizabeth's of finding Witchy mad; Peter's of Frank's drinking; John's of burying Veronica; Helen's prophetic dream of her father in jail.

That night John draws the task of killing Stutgard. Bad John goes ahead but is a poor lookout because darkness means nothing to him. He reports that another man has already killed Stutgard. Peter resents this intrusion upon the children’s intended victims, and they proceed to the house.

They proceed anyway, forming a human tower: Peter on Elizabeth's shoulders, Helen on Peter's, Dorothy on Helen's, Charles on Dorothy's. John climbs the swaying tower with his small axe "as sharp as a corn knife." But when he emerges, he carries a different axe: larger, heavier, a man's axe, bloody. Helen insists he killed Stutgard: "People who don't love blood don't love anything." But John admits Bad John was right. The Bagarthach to burn the shanty also fails. They return home confused, leave the bloody axe on the back porch, and go to bed under the same roof as Henry and Frank, their next intended victims.

Key Events

  • Decision to kill everyone in the world (Helen overrules Elizabeth and Charles's partial genocide)
  • Dorothy's Bagarthach to burn the Big Shanty (fails; countered by adults)
  • Swimming shows extraordinary speed and endurance underwater, with limits made explicit in Peter’s later distress.
  • Four parabolic stories told while drying on sycamore branches
  • Night mission: human tower formed to reach Stutgard's window
  • Bad John reports Stutgard already dead; children proceed anyway
  • John finds Stutgard murdered, retrieves Crocker's bloody axe
  • Big Shanty did not burn; the verse failed against the Kindred
  • Children leave axe on porch, sleep under same roof as their next targets

John's Story: The Onion and the Crate

A boy and his mother have only one onion to eat, eating one layer each night. When it's gone, the mother dies. The boy brings another onion, but she's been put in a crate. Her cold-iron grip catches his hand and won't let go. The men say they'll saw his arm off. "Get in or get out," they tell him. Unable to decide, they push him into the crate and bury him with his mother. (An account of Veronica's death and burial.)

Helen tells of a girl visiting her father in jail and trying to free him with a verse. She says Bagarthach often fails in a dream. The story anticipates Henry’s imprisonment and the children’s departure.

Chapter 4

And Six Were Kids, and One a Ghostie

Henry is arrested for Stutgard's murder; the children acquire the Île de France and prepare to take to the waters.

Summary

Shortly after midnight, Sheriff Train arrives with five "hasty deputies" to arrest Henry Dulanty for Stutgard's murder. The sheriff explains: "You aren't liked by the people here. They say you are neither fish nor flesh." Henry asks: "Must I be one or the other?" He could fight: "Did you ever arrest a goblin or ogre who stood six feet four and was as heavy as a yearling ox?" But Earth Allergy has made him listless. "This is their hour," he tells Frank, "and the power of darkness."

Henry tells Frank to put away the bottle and care for the children: the household has lost three adults in three days, to death, derangement, and arrest with intent to lynch. After the arrest, Elizabeth retrieves the bloody axe and hides it.

In the morning, Frank warns the children to stay close to the house. Helen defies him: "A welfare lady will come and take us away. They'll put us in a home, and we'll get stubborn and we won't eat, and we'll all be dead within a week." She notes that their Bagarthach to kill the adults failed. Frank explains: "We shot your Bagarthach down with another one. You can't use them against the Kindred."

The children visit Fulbert Fronsac in the flimsy Lost Haven jail, entering through the roof as they always do. Fulbert greets them: "Misere! There are days when the Good God is not good." He's one of the few who can see Bad John, whom he calls "Ange-Jacques." They acquire a deed to the Île de France; Peter writes it "in a man's hand," joking about the Salic Law, in exchange for a stolen bottle of whisky. Bad John signs with "a different kind of pencil," making a mark invisible to outsiders. The chapter ends with the children sailing away "to be gone forever."

Smoke from the dead’s chamber haunts the river bend. Helen and Peter dispute the meaning of left and right banks. Helen’s explanation is deliberately muddled; Peter answers with a joke about her brain.

Chapter 16

Or Altered World or Dead Dulanty

The children depart at moonrise; Witchy delivers her final charge; the Dragons' Seed is loosed upon the world.

Summary

Witchy arrives to give her parting admonition. Her charge shifts from killing everybody toward controlling and altering the world, though she still permits killing. Aorach Stories

"It was their way of defying that tricky place Earth... They whistled in the dark."

❡
On the Aorach

Aorach is a Puca narrative art, including ghost stories, accounts of family origins, and other kinds of tale. Irish aorach means satirical; aerach, a separate word used near the ending, can mean airy or lively.

"Listen, people, creatures, devices, entities, it was the meanest world in all the universes! It'd have spooked you too."

The Chapter 1 Stories

In Chapter 1, each of the six living children tells an Aorach story. Bad John cannot tell proper ghost stories: "he could only tell people stories, and nobody cares about them."

Elizabeth's Story: The Haunted Picture

▣
Theme: Careless Magic

Albert and his unnamed wife have a picture in which the man’s appearance changes. A dog then appears in it.

Frustrated with her husband, she makes a careless Bagarthach verse:

I'm turning livid in this bog,
This woolly world that spooks and spites you.
You'll find that picture's got a dog!
I hope the blinking bugger bites you!

The dog bites and kills the husband.

Moral: "Earth things are so dumb that they don't know when to obey a Bagarthach and when not to."

Charles's Story: The Country Hotel

⌂
Theme: Identity Dissolution

A Puca traveling salesman discovers Earth has been added to his territory. At a country hotel, strange things happen: a loud bang, lights turning on by themselves.

The tale’s shifting identities resolve into the hotel man’s corpse. The traveler escapes with a doorknob that becomes a rock, while the hotel disappears.

Theme: The instability of Earth reality; the impossibility of knowing what is real.

Helen's Story: Silly Jimmy

⚙
Theme: The Dead Who Don't Know

A man returns to his hometown and talks to Silly Jimmy by the railroad tracks. His family reminds him that Silly Jimmy died long ago, run over by a train. A mocking verse was made about him:

The engine spattered him like tar,
And broke his bones and burst his belly.
We gathered Jimmy in a jar.
Hey, pass the Silly Jimmy Jelly!

Yet fresh wood shavings prove Jimmy had been whittling, dead or not.

Theme: The persistence of the dead.

Peter's Story: Alice and Clarissa

❡
Theme: Trickery and Substitution

A girl named Alice dies and goes to Hell for stealing money, but it was really her sister Clarissa who did it. Alice returns as a ghost to scare Clarissa into confessing.

Clarissa takes Alice’s ghost sheet. The guard mistakes her for Alice and carries Clarissa to Hell, leaving Alice on Earth.

Moral: "A lot of times, you can make everything work all right if you send just one person out of a bunch to Hell."

Dorothy's Story: The Ghost Train

⚙
Theme: Haunted Ground

A man killed by a train returns once a year to swing a lantern on the tracks. One night he steals a real lantern, and a ghost train, the same one that killed him, switches onto the old track.

Dorothy says she makes a sign and the train passes through the shanty without breaking it up. It is not diverted away.

Theme: The Big Shanty itself is haunted ground; the children live over a ghost railroad.

John's Story: William and Polly

†
Theme: The Promise Kept

A Puca man (William) and Earth woman (Polly) marry and promise that whoever dies first will return to tell the other what death is like. William dies; Polly remarries Tom.

William comes three times to the door:

  • First time: "It's cold."
  • Second time: "It's dark."
  • Third time: "It's lonesome."

Polly explains the first visits away as those of the coal man and kerosene man, then rejects William’s news of loneliness. A wind kills Tom, freezes Polly, destroys the house, and buries her. The tale ends with William’s claim to be showing her what death is like.

Theme: Promises to the dead cannot be escaped.

Bad John's Problem

He couldn’t tell ghost stories, he could only tell people stories, and nobody cares about them.
— Narrator on Bad John

As a ghost himself, Bad John cannot tell proper ghost stories; he lacks the necessary distance from the subject. His "people stories" are dismissed by the other children.

The Parabolic Stories (Chapter 3)

In Chapter 3, four children tell stories while drying on tree branches after swimming. These are not traditional Aorach but parabolic accounts of recent traumas, told in oblique third person.

Elizabeth's Story: The Floozy

A thinly veiled account of finding Witchy gone mad. A little girl follows her mother's perfume through dark streets and finds "a crazy floozy." The floozy turns and says "My God, Elizabeth! That you should see me like this."

Reality: Elizabeth discovered Witchy's madness the night before.

Peter's Story: The Brown Bottle

A boy watches his father drink from a brown bottle. With each glass, the father changes: "like the man in the story that changed into a frog." His eyes go gray and glazed, his face reddens.

Connection: Peter’s story responds to Frank’s drinking and frightening transformation after Veronica’s death.

John's Story: The Onion and the Crate

A boy and his mother have only one onion to eat, eating one layer each night. When it's gone, the mother dies. The boy brings another onion, but she's been put in a crate. Her cold-iron grip catches his hand. Unable to decide whether to get in or get out, they push him into the crate and bury him with his mother.

Reality: Veronica's death and burial.

Helen's Story: Papa in Jail

Helen’s prison story anticipates Henry’s arrest and the children’s flight by boat. Her verse fails; she says Bagarthach often fails in a dream.

Reality: Foreshadows Henry's arrest and the children's departure on the Île de France.

Veronica's Characteristic Aorach

Referenced in Chapter 2, Veronica's signature story explains how she and Witchy were "twins" born eleven months apart:

Imagine an Earth doctor trying to tell a mistress of the Aorach about something as fundamental as that.
— Narrator on Veronica’s birth story

The narrator sides with Veronica’s mastery against the doctor’s objection.

Puca Nature

Biology, abilities, appearance, and the fatal curse of Earth Allergy.

◊
What Are the Puca?

The Pucas combine alien and fairy identities. Henry speculates about Neanderthals and describes off-Earth history; neither settles an origin. Earth Sickness weakens the adults, but it has no uniform timetable: Pandemonium John has worked his circuit for decades.

Appearance

The Dual Aspect

Puca can appear either human or goblin, and the Dulanty parents deliberately bred for both:

"Some of the children should look like children of this place, and some of them should look like the children back home."

Human-Appearing ("Like Angels")

  • Elizabeth: "The blackest hair and the whitest skin in the world." Beautiful as an Earth child.
  • Charles: Looks "like an angel."
  • Helen: Pretty, distinguishable only by gleaming eyes and ear-flicking.

Goblin-Appearing ("Like Real Pucas")

  • Peter: one of the goblin-appearing children.
  • Dorothy: Goblin-appearing.
  • John: The funniest-looking of all.
  • Bad John: Translucent goblin.

The Sideways Glimpse

"Look at them straight, and you'd hardly notice a thing. But look at them sideways accidentally sometimes, just catching them in the corner of your eye, and they'd scare the liver out of you."

Even the human-appearing children reveal their true nature when glimpsed indirectly. The lawyer in Chapter 12 sees "a veritable gargoyle" in his peripheral vision.

The Green Eyes

All Puca have green eyes that glow in the dark, a recurring motif in the novel:

"Six little pair of goblin eyes glowing green in the dark; and a seventh older pair nictitated by death slumber to a fainter green. And opposed to them, only the defenseless World!"

The Death Mask

In death, even human-appearing Puca reveal their alien nature:

"Henry's shot face had become somewhat unhuman in repose, but not less than human. The Dulantys could manage to look like regular people, till they had to laugh, or die."

Abilities

Bagarthach (Magical Verse)

See Bagarthach Verses for full treatment. Puca can kill, summon, curse, and transform through rhymed verse.

Physical Superiority

It was, of course, a war of the Worlds.
— Narrator on Henry’s fight with Marshall

Henry defeats Marshall in hand-to-hand combat despite Earth Allergy. Frank moves with supernatural speed to catch Crocker. The children display abilities beyond Earth children.

Telepathic Speech

The lawyer observes in Chapter 12:

They think out loud when they wish to do so, and vocal cords (if such they have) have nothing to do with it.
— The lawyer’s waking dream, as narrated

Aquatic Ability

In Chapter 3, the children swim in ways impossible for humans:

  • Staying underwater for extended periods
  • Swimming "like eels"
  • Comfort in water matching their home world ("land and water inextricably mixed")

Instinctive Music

Puca can play any instrument instinctively, as demonstrated during the concert with Pandemonium John.

Earth Allergy

☠
The Fatal Curse

Earth Sickness affects body, judgment, and disposition. The narrator first says the children suffered it lightly and acquired immunity; Peter’s later passion complicates this, while Witchy assures them at the end that it will not catch them. The text distinguishes youthful disturbance from the adults’ fatal decline and gives no medical system.

Symptoms

  • Flushing and "hot eye" (resembles drunkenness)
  • Wavering gait
  • Progressive weakness
  • Listlessness and loss of will
  • Tainting with "Earth outlook"
  • Death

Victims

  • Veronica: Dies early in the novel's chronology
  • Frank: Appears drunk but is suffering Earth Allergy; killed before it could claim him
  • Henry: Weakened by Earth Allergy; allows arrest; killed before it could claim him
  • Witchy: Dies of Earth Allergy in Chapter 16
  • Pandemonium John: "Old and tired and racked with Earth Allergy" but still alive

Frank's Condition

He wavered in his walk like a drunk, and he had the flushed look and the hot eye of a drunk. But he was sober as a Rogers County judge. It was the Earth Allergy that had caught up with him and sent him along reeling and sick, that would kill him soon if something else didn’t do it first.
— Narrator on Frank

Ghost Children

Puca children who die may persist as ghosts, as Bad John demonstrates:

  • Translucent (visible and see-through simultaneously)
  • Invisible/inaudible to most Earth people
  • Can pass through walls and unlock doors
  • Cannot write, though can make marks some people see
  • Doesn't eat
  • Can see other ghosts (and be punched by them)
  • Eventually departs ("gone for good")
"He's no more a ghost than you are. He's scaredest of all of us of ghosts because he can see them oftenest, and they can punch him like they can't do it to us. Bad John is just different because he died when he was a baby."

Puca and Indians

The Puca were told they would be "in rapport with the Indian population," but their information was "centuries out of date." Despite this, mutual recognition persists:

  • Men at the Lost Moon Bar recognize Pucas through grandmother stories.
  • Phoebe Jane Lanyard can "be as goblin as they could"
  • The children find kinship at Misu Mound ("a mountainful of murdered Indians")
  • Indians and Puca share an expression "when they have just killed someone"

Puca and Dogs

"There is an ancient antipathy between Pucas and every sort of dogs, but especially the great hounds."

The narrator first states a broad antipathy between Pucas and dogs, especially hounds. Henry’s search for a farm then distinguishes indifferent, frightened, hostile, and relatively reasonable dogs.

❧
Kobolds and the Underground

The narrator eventually distinguishes Pucas from goblins by saying that they look more like kobolds. The German folkloric term can name household or underground spirits. Its mining associations fit the strip pits and mound tunnels; the novel’s Puca history is its own invention.

Cosmology

The Four Worlds, the Hive Ghost, Dragons' Seed, and the theological underpinnings of Lafferty's vision.

The Four Worlds

🌍
Earth as One of Four

Earth is called one of the Four Worlds, but the set is not exhaustively identified. Mercator, Astrobe, and Camiroi are named in other contexts; Pandemonium John also speaks of a dozen worlds. The home landscape imagined by the children mixes land and water.

The Mission

The adult Dulantys were sent to Earth by Puca "instructors" as "picked young couples" with a dual mandate:

  1. Primary: "Achieve accord with Earth": integrate with humanity
  2. Further task: consider whether something could be done with Earth, an ambition whose implications become increasingly coercive.

The mission has failed to secure accord. The children inherit both the ambition to alter Earth and the unresolved question of how to deal with its inhabitants; the final charge does not specify a second mission.

The Hive Ghost

🐝
Earth's Collective Consciousness

The Puca observe that Earth people operate with a collective intelligence they cannot understand:

"The Hive Ghost hovers over them invisible, and they feel it from one end of their communities to the other. We can handle Earth individuals as though they were furniture or stones, but we cannot handle the Hive Ghost that hangs over them."
— Frank Dulanty

Properties

  • Earth people act with "lightning instinct": faster than thought
  • They possess "unity and communication of the hive"
  • Frank describes a collective instinct that, to him, works faster than thought.
  • Individual Earth people are manageable; the collective is not

This explains how Lost Haven instinctively rejects the Dulantys: the townspeople sense the difference even when they cannot articulate it. "We sense you here as they wouldn't sense you in a larger or busier place."

Dragons' Seed

🐉
"A Time Bomb in You"

Henry reveals to Marshall that the Puca have left something behind on Earth: "dragons' eggs" that have "hatched and are going now":

"You'll not become dominant here. We'll kill you off like the damned dragons you are."

"Yes, I begin to think that you will. But you won't win by it. We've laid dragons' eggs behind us. They're hatched and going now. You'll not know the young while they're growing. They are a time bomb in you."

Interpretations

  • Children and successors: Henry’s image suggests Puca continuance beyond the adults’ deaths.
  • Metaphorical: Ideas, arts, or cultural influences that will transform Earth
  • The Children: The six Dulanty children themselves, released by Witchy's charge

Witchy's Release

In Chapter 16, Witchy explicitly names the children as Dragons' Seed:

"I break the egg! I turn you loose! Oh, my own Dragons' Seed!"

The Primitive Ethic vs. Earth Ethics

The "Peculiar and Remarkable" Earth Ethic

Witchy describes an Earth ethic (implicitly Christian) that the Puca find both admirable and impossible:

"There is on this world a most peculiar and remarkable ethic. The people of this world fail it by not taking it literally enough. Ourselves, if we try it, will fail it by taking it too literally."

She tells of a Puca cousin who followed it literally ("If your right hand offend you, hack it off") until "there was nothing left of him but the torso."

The Puca Primitive Ethic

"Ourselves, we live by a more primitive ethic. We do not deny that it is primitive, but it is the only ethic that the Giver has given us yet. We live that primitive ethic all the way. Be swift, and be sudden! That is our secret."

Witchy offers the primitive ethic as the Pucas’ received rule while acknowledging the demands of another ethic. Her permission to kill and her preference for control and alteration are morally difficult, and Frank’s undelivered appeal to gentleness runs against them.

The Haunted Planet

Earth is consistently portrayed as a haunted, hostile place: "the meanest world in all the universes." The Aorach stories of Chapter 1 establish Earth as a place where:

  • Pictures come alive and kill
  • Hotels vanish and identities blur
  • The dead don't know they're dead
  • Ghost trains run beneath houses
  • Death cannot be escaped through promises

The Reefs of Earth

The novel's title comes from the Heroic Chantey in Chapter 14:

"Stranded Like a Fool, Dulanty—
On the Reefs of Earth—Untimely—"

Earth is a reef, a hidden danger that wrecks travelers. The Dulantys are stranded on it, dying of Earth Allergy, persecuted by the natives, unable to return home.

Puca Origins

The Neanderthal Theory

Henry hints that the Puca may have been Earth's original intelligent species:

"There is enmity between our flesh from the beginning. We fought before, in an ice cave a million years ago, either on Earth or off it. You're an almost-man."
— Marshall to Henry

Henry’s Neanderthal suggestion is speculative. Marshall’s account of an ancient fight in an ice cave expresses his sense of primordial enmity.

The Museum Hoax

Henry’s museum prank places a deceased kinsman, labeled Puca Man, beyond the representation of Modern Man. The joke asserts a superior place in the evolutionary display.

Catholic Elements

Lafferty’s work engages Catholic questions. In this novel the theological claims are made by particular speakers:

Luke's Gospel

Henry quotes Luke 22:53 before his arrest:

“But this is their hour,” said Henry, “and the power of darkness.”
— Henry

The Giver

Witchy refers to "the Giver" as the source of the Puca's primitive ethic, a divine being who has given them a different moral law than Earth people received.

Apotheosis

Dorothy’s verse calls the proposed fiery deaths an Apotheosis. The grand word turns murder into mock glorification.

Nil de Monstris Nisi Bonum

The Heroic Chantey's final line, "Nothing about monsters except good," parallels the Latin maxim nil de mortuis nisi bonum ("nothing about the dead except good"). It asks that only good be said of the Dulantys as monsters.

The Final Image

"Six little pair of goblin eyes glowing green in the dark; and a seventh older pair nictitated by death slumber to a fainter green.

And opposed to them, only the defenseless World!"

The ending places six living children and their dying mother against the world. The comic reversal draws sympathy to the Dulantys while the threat of their violence stays in view.

Themes

Readings of the novel’s violence, comedy, art, and damaged world.

✦The Two Plots

The children’s war and the adults’ persecution proceed in different registers, but their objects and agents connect them. The stolen axe becomes legal evidence. Crocker’s hired captors carry the pursuit into the children’s refuge. Fulbert’s signal brings Charles into the last hunt, while Phoebe supplies the shot Charles mistakes for his own.

✦The Business of Lost Haven

Marshall describes roughly a thousand relief checks in a town with roughly three hundred people: the dead, departed, and invented stay profitable. False crop histories and mined land support another stream of payments; land obtained for a dollar an acre adds to the scheme. Stutgard’s persecution and Marshall’s ambition thus have material interests as well as racial hatred behind them. The Dulantys’ own dubious business sales complicate any clean division between corrupt humans and economically innocent Pucas.

✦Violence and the Child’s Perspective

The children intend extermination and attempt particular killings. The Stinkers, Amada, and Fiddler escape through accidents, deception, and agility; Phoebe’s hospitality creates a different obstacle. The offstage fate of the blackbirders is especially grave: real ears, a real silencer, and Dorothy’s complete accusation weigh against a sentimental reading.

Veronica dies of Earth Allergy, not at human hands. Human persecution is real, but the novel does not endorse extermination as the answer to it. The narration’s participation in the children’s perspective is itself part of the moral problem.

✦Frank’s Unspoken Counsel and Witchy’s Charge

Frank intends to tell the children to deal gently with the world and seek a broader way. His murder prevents the speech. Witchy’s delivered admonition permits killing yet shifts their immediate purpose from killing everybody to controlling and altering. Her thought about responsibility escapes her. Together the two counsels leave an ethical argument unfinished.

✦Indigenous Alliance and Puca Ambition

Phoebe Jane, John Loose Screw, Sammy Bluefield, and the dead of the mound offer forms of recognition denied by Lost Haven’s rulers. This affinity is not identity. The Puca have their own projects of dominance and speak of becoming an elite; their original mission is not established as flight from an expulsion at home. Witchy is an institutionalized adult, while removal of the children is still only a threatened action. These differences bear on the colonial analogy.

✦Art, Refuge, and the Damaged Landscape

The strip pits are industrial wreckage and a child’s terrain of invention, an image of home and a battlefield. Misu Mound provides refuge among the dead while preserving competing stories and deep layers of burial. The honkeroo makes artistic joy inseparable from monstrous lyrics. An Oklahoma Gothic reading sits beside a pastoral one, concerned with what home, art, and freedom can be made in a damaged place.

✦Faith, Literalism, and the Anointed

Henry’s theology differs from the priest’s despite his scriptural learning. He approaches the Bible as Earth mythology and says the Puca do not generally believe the Anointed has yet been born. The priest asks whether salvation reaches intelligent aliens. Witchy’s literal reading of Christian commands, her appeal to a primitive ethic, and her fading responsibility keep the theological difficulty open. Dorothy’s word Apotheosis makes a murderous joke.

✦Story, Satire, and Authority

Aorach includes more than ghost tales. Veronica’s birth story defeats the doctor’s objection in the narrator’s telling; Pandemonium’s family songs are called literal. Yet Helen’s inventions are challenged, Phoebe’s Puca burial layer is rejected, and professors’ certainty is mocked. The novel tests who can tell the truth and what a literal account can contain.

✦Fairy Terror and the Final Reversal

Irish Pooka tradition already includes frightening and coercive encounters. His Pucas add extraterrestrial history, illness, art, and family loyalties to that difficult inheritance. The final defenseless World is a comic reversal of power whose promise and threat stay together. The four ABAB quatrains of chapter titles announce the move toward alteration; the book ends before the children’s later rule can be judged.

For the folklore comparison, see the Dúchas records in Sources & Editorial Notes.

❧
Allusion as Part of the Action

Borrowed language repeatedly becomes an instrument or a difficulty within the plot. Peter turns Laocoon’s warning into a prediction about the murder weapon. Marshall uses Gogol to explain profitable fraud, then treats literary knowledge as a possession that should give him mastery. Henry answers with a verse whose promised rifle will fire after his death. Scripture is quoted, altered, falsely attributed, argued over, and taken disastrously literally. The references do work in the story.

Sources: Virgil, Aeneid 2.49; Gogol, Dead Souls, part 1, ch. 11.

Glossary

Key terms, Puca vocabulary, regional language, and specialized concepts.

Puca Terms

Aorach (possibly from Irish aoire or aerach)

Puca narrative art, broader than ghost stories. Irish aorach means satirical; aerach is a distinct word. See Aorach Stories.

Bagarthach (possibly from Irish bagairt, "threat")

Puca verse with power to act on bodies and events. Irish bagarthach is a form referred to bagrach, threatening. The novel shows counterverses and disputed limits. See Bagarthach Verses.

Dragons' Seed

What Witchy calls the children when releasing them in Chapter 16. Also refers to "dragons' eggs" Henry mentions, meaning Puca influences left on Earth as "a time bomb in you." See Cosmology.

Earth Allergy

The illness of Pucas on Earth. Early statements of the children’s immunity coexist with Peter’s later distress and Witchy’s final reassurance. The adults’ decline has no fixed timetable. See Puca Nature.

Four Worlds, The

Earth is one of four, but the four are not securely enumerated. Other passages name Mercator, Astrobe, and Camiroi and mention a dozen worlds.

Hive Ghost

The Puca term for Earth's collective human consciousness. "The Hive Ghost hovers over them invisible... We can handle Earth individuals as though they were furniture or stones, but we cannot handle the Hive Ghost that hangs over them." See Cosmology.

Kindred

Other Pucas. Frank invokes Kindred while explaining the counterverse that defeated Dorothy’s spell. An inset tale complicates a universal claim of immunity.

Primitive Ethic

The moral code the Puca follow, distinct from Earth's "peculiar and remarkable ethic" (Christianity). "We live by a more primitive ethic... Be swift, and be sudden! That is our secret."

Puca (from Irish púca)

The alien/fairy species to which the Dulantys belong. Can appear human or goblin. Possess magical abilities through Bagarthach. In Irish folklore, the púca is a shape-shifting creature associated with harvest time and trickery.

Puca Sleep Hold

A technique for rendering someone unconscious. Henry uses it on the jailer during his escape: "He throttled the jailer very gently and stretched him out to sleep on the floor."

The Giver

Witchy's term for the divine being who gave the Puca their primitive ethic. "The only ethic that the Giver has given us yet."

Lost Moon Bar

The bar where Indigenous men recognize and help Henry during his escape.

Misu Mound

Burial mound with contested histories. Helen estimates thirty thousand dead. The children find kinship there; Helen later exchanges a buffalo thighbone for a dead woman’s own bone.

Strip Pits

"A dozen square miles of mountains turned inside out, threaded by canals, and with bottomless chasms." The remnants of surface coal mining, now the children's domain. See The Strip Pits.

Fulbert’s fishing raft, deeded to the children for whisky. Its grand name suits his royal pretensions; a specific ocean-liner allusion is possible but unconfirmed.

Sea Bear

The Stinkers' raft, captured during the Battle of Port Stinker. Towed behind the Île de France.

Abhacht, Puca

Irish ábhacht concerns jest or drollery. Lafferty’s parenthesis about unearthliness extends the word’s comic sense in the description of Witchy.

Accord

The Dulanty families were assigned to achieve accord with Earth and its people. After more than ten years, this accord had decidedly not been achieved, leading Henry Dulanty to speculate whether Earth must adapt to the Puca instead.

Addresses

Not kept by Pucas for inter-group communication; contact relies instead on "informal and intuitive" methods or directed Bagarthach verses. Witchy requires Phoebe Jane Lanyard to use Shawnee senses to locate other Pucas like Willy McGilly or Diogenes Pontifex.

Admonition, Parting

A final instruction given by a Puca before death or departure. Witchy delivers one before her expected death, tasking the children with living by the primitive Puca ethic ("Be swift, and be sudden!") to overcome the "many fat rats" of the world.

Advice

Veronica Dulanty advised the children against prolonged underwater stays to avoid suspicion, advice unheeded after her death. Pandemonium John offers advice against taking cover from unpleasantness, urging the Puca children to "soak in them and luxuriate" instead. Helen cries "The advice! It's coming" as if it were a physical force emanating from Pandemonium John himself.

Aerach

The concluding outre scene of a classic Puca drama, characterized by excess, the heaping together of bloody events, and a tone both tragic and burlesque. The final confrontation involving Crocker, Frank, Marshall, and Charles is presented as such a scene. See also Aorach Story.

Albert

The Puca man in Elizabeth's cautionary tale about Earth's haunted nature. His wife reminds him to dispose of the cursed picture containing the blood-foaming dog that killed him, forgetting in the moment that he is dead.

Alice

In Peter’s story, Alice has been sent to Hell for a theft committed by Clarissa. Alice returns in a ghost sheet; Clarissa takes the sheet, and the guard carries Clarissa away. Alice stays on Earth.

Alien

Used reciprocally between Pucas and humans to denote fundamental difference and otherness. Henry Dulanty identifies himself as alien to Earth but later reverses the perspective, calling the priest the alien. Frank Dulanty is described by Pandemonium John as being in a race against "the alien creatures who look like people." Fulbert Fronsac refers to the "multiplex alien mind" of the Puca children.

Allegory

The narrator insists that Puca arts are literal accounts of literal happenings, especially when describing Pandemonium John’s family songs.

Allergy, Earth

Also "Earthen Sickness" or "Earth Sickness." A seemingly inescapable malady affecting adult Pucas living on Earth, characterized by physical deterioration, listlessness, emotional volatility or madness, a morbid Earth-centric outlook, and the dulling of Puca senses and vitality. Children born on Earth generally develop immunity after suffering it lightly. It proves fatal for Veronica, contributes to Witchy's institutionalization, and severely affects Frank and Henry. Frank uses alcohol as a "Specific against" it. See Puca Nature.

Almost-Man

A descriptor emphasizing the Puca's near-human yet fundamentally different nature. The priest perceives Henry Dulanty as an "almost-man" who flicks his ears like an animal. Mandrake Marshall also refers to Henry as an "almost-man."

Altered World

The potential outcome of the Dulanty children's presence and actions on Earth. Represents the alternative to "Dead Dulanty," suggesting a fundamental transformation of the planet according to Puca ways rather than achieving accord with its existing state.

Amhranai, Eigeas-

A Puca term signifying a high master of song and verse, applied to Pandemonium John. The term suggests a level of artistry and power beyond Earthly comprehension, incorporating elements of epic poetry, diverse musical traditions, and raw, primal performance.

Ancestors

The state into which the Dulanty children conceptualize their parents transitioning after death or incapacitation. Helen terms this change "metamorphosis," a change of status.

Ange-Jacques

Fulbert Fronsac's name for the ghost-child Bad John. Fulbert is one of the few humans who can perceive Bad John. Testimony based on Ange-Jacques's account is deemed intangible by Fulbert himself.

Angels

Mentioned in relation to the Dulanty children's appearance and Puca cosmology. Some children (Elizabeth, Charles, Helen) initially look like angels or handsome Earthlings. Henry Dulanty claims Pucas sometimes judge Angels when they fall within Puca jurisdiction, referencing Earth scripture (Paul) to the priest's surprise.

Anima and Matrix

Latin anima, soul, and matrix, womb. The Puca Anima in an Earth Matrix is the image used to describe Peter’s violent, misdirected yearnings.

Animal, Unknown

The professors explain the anomalous skeleton as an assemblage of familiar animal parts, with cue-ball fangs. That is their debunking account. The narrator mocks their refusal to excavate the mound themselves.

Anointed, The

Discussed by Henry Dulanty and the priest. Henry relates the concept to the Puca term Fear-ungadh-mac and notes the Puca teaching that the Anointed will be born on the "meanest of worlds," possibly Earth.

Anomaly

Frank Dulanty's term for the nature of Earth people. He finds them anomalous for operating via "frightening instinct" and hive communication rather than individual intelligence.

Anthropological Museum

Where a Puca specimen, the body of a deceased kinsman of Henry Dulanty, was briefly and prankishly displayed in a case labeled "Puca Man" just beyond the effigy of Modern Man.

Antipathy

An ancient and mutual hostility existing between Pucas and "every sort of dogs, but especially the great hounds." This inherent conflict makes hounds a particular danger during the manhunt for Henry Dulanty.

Aorach Story

One of the two Puca art-forms, both verbal; the other being the Bagarthach verse. Veronica Dulanty is identified as a mistress of the Aorach. She employs one such story, involving the simultaneous conception and subsequent eleven-month-apart births of herself and her sister Witchy, to explain their relationship, baffling an Earth doctor with its disregard for conventional biology. See Aorach Stories.

Ape

Mandrake Marshall's derogatory term for Henry Dulanty ("masquerading ape," "murdering ape"), signifying his perception of Henry as subhuman, animalistic, and fundamentally other. It expresses Marshall's prejudice and justifies, for him, hunting Henry.

Apotheosis

The state Dorothy Dulanty's Bagarthach verse assigns to the elder Dulantys after proposing to "kindle them in flames like roses." In this context, it signifies transformation through death by fire.

Appearance (Puca/Earth)

The distinct physical characteristics of Pucas and their attempts to resemble Earth people. Pucas are generally "not handsome," often appearing as "potato-faced goblins" or ogre-like, though some possess exceptional or uncanny beauty. Maturity brings a "deeper face, intricate and ugly." Key Puca tells include glowing green eyes and the flicking of ears. Pucas can choose to look more like Earth people but often deem it not "worth the bother."

Armada, Dulanty

The term used to describe the two rafts, Île de France and Sea Bear, when operated jointly by the Dulanty children during their campaign in the strip pits.

Assignment

The task given to the Dulanty families upon coming to Earth: to learn local custom, be gainfully employed, and perhaps determine if "something could not be done with Earth." This involves surviving, studying the planet and its people, and raising their Earth-born children.

Assumption, Presumptive

The Lost Moon Bar’s method of assuming that a newcomer will buy drinks for everyone. The narrator locates its roots between a Pragmatic Sanction, a sovereign decree, and Kant’s Categorical Imperative, an unconditional moral command. The inflated comparison makes social pressure sound like political and moral necessity.

Astrobe

Henry names an inhabitant of Astrobe among possible recipients of a Puca confession, after a Sagart and another Puca. The novel gives no complete list of the Four Worlds.

Atmosphere

Considered by Witchy to be insufficiently "nourishing" for the ghost-child Bad John on Earth, prompting his departure with his father Frank's spirit. Implies Puca spirits require conditions not fully met by Earth's environment.

Atropos

The Fate who cuts the thread of life. Marshall casts himself in that role toward Henry. Henry calls her oldest and ugliest.

Attitude

The non-verbal means by which the goat Catherine de Medici communicates direction, indicating the hidden location of Fiddler's Landing to the Dulanty children.

Automobile Horn

A squeeze-bulb horn fitted to the raft Île de France. Used frequently by Helen for signaling or challenges and by Dorothy as a distress signal.

Avunculi

Latin plural of avunculus, a maternal uncle. The narrator uses it during the children’s plan to kill their parents and uncles. The two brothers have married two sisters, so the adult men are linked to the children through both sides of the family.

A-wa-wa-shingay

A Shawnee phrase exchanged between the Dulanty children and Phoebe Jane Lanyard upon parting, seemingly signifying farewell or acknowledgment. It represents "all the Shawnee words they knew."

A-wa-way

Phoebe Jane Lanyard's soft reply to the Dulantys' cry of "A-wa-wa-shingay," possibly a reciprocal farewell or acknowledgment in the Shawnee language.

Axe

John Dulanty possesses a small axe, "sharp as a corn knife," intended for killing Mr. Stutgard. However, Stutgard is already dead, killed by Crocker with his own larger axe. John takes Crocker's bloody axe from the scene, leaving his own behind. Crocker's axe, marked with a "big C," is the murder weapon and is later hidden by Elizabeth in a potato sack.

B

Bad Blood

The inherent antagonism and troubled relationship existing between Pucas and Earth people, despite their being described as "cousins in blood." Mandrake Marshall also refers to the primal enmity between his kind and Henry Dulanty's.

Bad John (Ionuin)

The seventh Dulanty child, often uncounted, explicitly called the "ghost child." He died as a baby. He is translucent, invisible and inaudible to most, except individuals like Fulbert Fronsac (who calls him Ange-Jacques). He can operate unseen, unlatching windows from inside, but cannot whistle or tell ghost stories. He requires no food or bed. He witnesses Stutgard's murder by Crocker. His true Puca name is Ionuin. He departs Earth with the spirit of his father Frank, finding the Earth atmosphere unnourishing for his state. See Bad John.

Bad John's Mark

The distinctive, "queer mark" made by Bad John on the deed transferring ownership of the Île de France. Unlike standard writing, it was made with a "different kind of pencil" and visible only to Pucas and Fulbert Fronsac, unseen by Earth people like the lawyer.

Bagarthach Verse

A Puca verbal art whose verses can act on bodies and events. Frank credits a counterverse with defeating Dorothy’s spell; Helen says verses often fail in dreams. Irish bagarthach means threatening. Rosalind’s Irish-rat joke in As You Like It 3.2 recalls the tradition of Irish rhymers whose words could harm.

Sources: Shakespeare, As You Like It 3.2.

Bait

Helen claims that boys’ brains make catfish bait, but actual use is not established; Charles later wonders whether it would work. Elizabeth does use fish innards to fake the Stinkers’ severed ears. The Fabers’ alleged use of children as bait is expressly a rumour without evidence.

Balance

Emotional and psychological equilibrium that adult Pucas are in danger of losing due to Earth Sickness. Peter is "out of balance" from the sickness.

Band, Gallant

A descriptor, likely self-conceived by the Dulanty children or used ironically by the narrator, for their group, particularly regarding their ambitious plan to eliminate all other inhabitants of Earth. Used in the title of Chapter 3, "High Purpose of the Gallant Band."

Bank-Angle Analyzer

A hypothetical automotive device suggested by Henry Dulanty to Sammy Bluefield, designed to allow a car to navigate turns automatically.

Barataria Bay

In Louisiana, cited by university professors as the origin of alligator neck bones found in the anomalous composite skeleton presented by Phoebe Jane Lanyard's uncle.

Barman

The attendant at the Lost Moon Bar, an Indian who recognizes Henry Dulanty from tribal "Grandmother Stories."

Barnabas

One of Crocker's four hounds. He trails away during Crocker's final hunt, abandoning the disintegrating man.

Barren

Adjective describing Earth, implying a lack of cultural or spiritual life from a Puca perspective, a view contested by Pandemonium John who finds evidence of "real boondock music."

Barriers

Particularly the metaphysical barrier between life and death which the dying Henry Dulanty's powerful presence seems capable of transcending.

Barter

A form of trade proposed by Phoebe Jane Lanyard, exchanging her provisions (pecans, grape juice, hominy, deer meat) for the Dulanty children's captured keg of corn whisky.

Base

Charles Dulanty declares the children need a "base of operations, a moving one," leading to the acquisition of the Île de France.

"Battle Joined That Could But Leave"

The title of Chapter 15, marking the start of the final, inescapable conflicts involving Crocker, Frank Dulanty, and Mandrake Marshall, where survival for all parties is impossible.

Bayou

The children swim and dive in the deep bayou near the Big Shanty. Helen mentions Mud Bayou as part of the route to the sea.

Bear

Misu Mound includes animal burials. Crocker’s former hounds Matthew and Mark belong to two earlier accounts: one involving a boar coon, the other a young fast bear.

Beast Killer

The heroic persona Mandrake Marshall intends to cultivate by killing Henry Dulanty, whom he regards as a beast or ape. He expects this act will make the populace love him.

Beautiful Letters

A literal rendering of French belles-lettres. Marshall boasts of his repertory of literary and other knowledge. Henry’s reply tests that possession against Bagarthach verse that can alter events.

Bell

One of Crocker's three large mastiff hounds. Along with Book, he abandons Crocker during the final, boggy stages of the hunt.

Bell, Book, and Candle

The names of Crocker’s three mastiffs recall the traditional formula for solemn excommunication. Luke and John bear evangelists’ names; the absent Matthew and Mark complete that sequence, while Barnabas and Timothy recall apostolic companions.

Sources: John A. Hardon, “Bell-Book-Candle,” Modern Catholic Dictionary.

Belled

Marked so that an enemy can be tracked or confronted, as in Aesop’s proposal to bell the cat. Helen’s curse anticipates Frank’s public placement of the noose around Crocker’s neck. The same rope returns at the bog.

Sources: Aesop, “Belling the Cat”.

Bevanish

A unique verb meaning to vanish utterly and completely; used to describe how the "shattering profundity" of Puca maturity will "smash and bevanish" any youthful prettiness.

Big Boys

Term for older, larger Earth male children. Charles Dulanty targets three named "big boys" (Elwood Elgin, Rex Remagen, Barney Bottleby) for elimination. The blackbirders hunting the Dulantys are led by two "really big boys" aged eleven.

Big Shanty, The

The large, dilapidated building near the railroad tracks in Lost Haven, rented by Henry Dulanty from an Indian lady in Catoosa. It is the Dulanty family home, the setting for evening storytelling, the target of the children's failed arson Bagarthach, the location of Henry's arrest, and the site from which Crocker obtains Bad John's clothes for his hounds. See Lost Haven.

"Birmingham Jail Blues"

An American folk or blues song, performed by the Dulantys and Pandemonium John during their honkeroo as one of the "great songs of Earth."

Birth

The Dulanty children's birth on Earth is significant, granting citizenship and supposed immunity to Earth Allergy. The unusual circumstances of Veronica and Witchy's births are related in an Aorach story. The birthplace of the Anointed is a subject of theological discussion.

Blackbirders

Children hired by Crocker to find and seize the Dulantys for a dollar a day. Blackbirding denotes coerced recruitment or trafficking, particularly in the South Pacific; impressors, the companion word, recalls forced recruitment into service. Their names are Baxter Bushyhead, Little Sad Sam Jones, and Harold Harvestman.

Sources: Merriam-Webster, “blackbird”; Merriam-Webster, “impress”.

Blind Ben's Bar

A drinking establishment in Lost Haven where Frank Dulanty confronts Crocker's mob, enters with "lion-swiftness," and publicly humiliates Crocker with a noose. See Chapter 7.

Blood

Flows throughout the novel: from the dog's blood-foaming jaws in Elizabeth's story, to the crow's black juice, to Crocker's bloody axe, to Henry's murder. The Dulantys and humans are "cousins in blood" despite their enmity.

Bog

Crocker sinks when Marshall’s blow causes Frank to release the rope. Marshall rolls Frank into the bog. Charles later rolls Marshall in. Helen’s earlier curse finds its fulfillment in Crocker’s drowning in something other than water.

Book

One of Crocker's three mastiff hounds (Bell, Book, and Candle). Along with Bell, he abandons Crocker during the final hunt.

Boondocks

A remote backcountry; the English word comes from Tagalog bundok, mountain. The Puca use it for Earth itself, making the whole planet provincial from the travelers’ point of view.

Sources: Merriam-Webster, “boondocks”.

Briochtog

Witchy’s Puca name. Dinneen records briochtóg as witch or sorceress. She has held it longer than the children have been alive.

Brood, Gallant

Variant of "Band, Gallant." Used in the title of Chapter 9: "No Setting for the Gallant Brood."

Butterfield, Thomas

The farmer near Catoosa who arrests Henry and telephones the authorities. Marshall kills him after the killing of Henry, despite Butterfield’s appeal to his former vote for Marshall.

C

Camiroi

A world named in the account of a critic of Puca music and in Henry’s hierarchy of possible confessors. It is not securely identified as one particular member of a complete list of the Four Worlds.

Candle

One of Crocker's three mastiff hounds (Bell, Book, and Candle). Noted for blazing at night, Candle is the last dog left with Crocker after Bell and Book desert him in the bog. Marshall claims Candle after killing Frank.

Captain

Charles Dulanty's role aboard the Île de France. "Charles was captain. He had matured in one afternoon."

Carraig

Peter’s Puca name: Irish carraig, rock.

Catherine de Medici

Fulbert Fronsac's she-goat, described as being of royal blood, the great-great-granddaughter of the historical Queen of France. She lives primarily on the Île de France, understands some French, and communicates direction through "attitude."

Catoosa

Residence of the Indian lady who owns the Big Shanty. Also the general area of Thomas Butterfield's farmstead ("On a Farmstead Near Catoosa—").

Checks, Relief

Government assistance payments forming the basis of Coalfactor Stutgard's scheme to depopulate Lost Haven while fraudulently collecting benefits for non-existent or departed residents ("dead souls"). Marshall and Crocker received "jackals' shares" of this income: "bogus relief checks might run around sixty thousand dollars a month."

Choc Beer

Oklahoma homemade beer with varied recipes and a history involving several communities. Tobacco occurs in one 1894 account reported by the Oklahoma Historical Society; it is not a necessary ingredient of all choc beer. Jack Fiddler makes it in the novel.

Chosky Bottoms

The dusty low ground through which Pandemonium John approaches, printed in lower case in the novel. Lafferty later used the name in “Happening in Chosky Bottoms,” published in Kim Mohan’s Amazing Stories anthology in 1995. The recurring name is a useful cross-reference; an exact identification with a real Oklahoma locality is unproved.

Sources: Kirkus Reviews, Amazing Stories, ed. Kim Mohan (1995).

Clarissa

Clarissa committed the theft for which Alice was sent to Hell. She takes Alice’s ghost sheet, and the guard carries Clarissa to Hell by mistake; Alice stays on Earth.

Coelo-Syria

Henry’s geographical label for the anticipated birthplace of the Anointed. His small town recalls Bethlehem in Micheas (Micah) 5:2. Coele-Syria can have broader historical uses, but in its narrow geographical sense means the valley between Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon, not Bethlehem.

Cogent Person

Someone suitable to receive a Puca's confession, according to Henry Dulanty's hierarchy: an Earthman ranks above a dromedary or stone, but below a Puca or inhabitants of Astrobe or Camiroi.

Coisreacan-Dia

Elizabeth’s Puca name. Dinneen’s vocabulary of blessing and consecration supports a possible sense of consecration to God. “Footstep/Relic of God” is unsupported; the whole compound is not a standard dictionary headword.

Confession

Henry’s list proceeds from a Sagart to an ordinary Puca, other rational peoples, animals, a tree, and a stone. The Summa allows confession to a layperson in necessity, without priestly absolution; Henry’s list goes much further. The old priest hears him and dies a month later, rather than on the spot.

Sources: Aquinas, Summa theologiae, Supplement, q. 8, a. 2.

Coon-shouter

A historical label for a performer of so-called coon songs, a popular-song category named with an anti-Black slur. The narrator includes it in the catalogue of Earth musicians who, even combined, would fall short of Pandemonium John.

Sources: Merriam-Webster, “coon song”.

Corbie

A Scots word for a crow or raven. Helen’s verse calls down a crow for a raw meal. Its archaic diction contrasts with the poem’s ungenteel last word.

Sources: Merriam-Webster, “corbie”.

Cousins in Blood

The fundamental but troubled relationship between Pucas and Earth people. Despite being "cousins in blood," there exists inherent antagonism and "bad blood" between them.

Crocker

A primary antagonist in Lost Haven. Murders Coalfactor Stutgard with his distinctive axe. Hires blackbirders to hunt the children. Is terrified by Bad John witnessing the murder. Dies by Helen's Bagarthach curse: hanged over quicksand by Frank's noose. See Crocker.

Crocker's Axe

The large, distinctive axe marked with a "big C," used to murder Stutgard. Taken from the scene by John Dulanty in error and hidden by Elizabeth in a potato sack, becoming crucial evidence.

Crow Creek

A stream flowing through or near the strip pits. The Dulantys navigate it during their escape.

D

Dark Wood

Henry’s flight repeats the opening situation and much of the wording of Dante’s Inferno: he comes to himself in a dark wood where the straight way is lost. John Loose Screw guides him to help. The verbal echo is secure.

Sources: Dante, Inferno 1.1–3, Carlyle translation as revised.

Dead Dulanty

The alternative stated by the last chapter title: “Or Altered World or Dead Dulanty.” It states the outcome as a choice between two alternatives.

Dead Souls

Gogol’s novel, invoked through the name Pavel Ivanovitch, or Chichikov. His plan monetizes dead serfs still on official records; Stutgard’s version extracts relief payments for people who have died, departed, or never existed. The agricultural payments based on false crop histories are an additional fraud.

Sources: Gogol, Dead Souls, part 1, ch. 11.

Demiurge

A world-maker or divine craftsman in philosophical and religious traditions. Henry initially wonders whether the figure emerging from the ground is one. The man is John Lewis Grew, called John Loose Screw. The first appearance is printed demiurage; the following page has demiurges.

Dia-Ta-Caomh

John’s Puca name. A plausible intended sense is that God is kind or gracious; caomh includes dear and gentle. The compound is not a standard dictionary phrase.

Diogenes Pontifex

A Puca mentioned by Witchy as someone Phoebe Jane might locate using her Shawnee senses. The name combines the Greek philosopher with Latin "bridge-builder/priest."

Dogs

The novel asserts an ancient antipathy between Pucas and dogs, especially great hounds. In Crocker’s final hunt Barnabas leaves, then the other three hounds, then Book and Bell; Candle is left.

Dragons' Eggs

Henry’s image of Puca successors already hatching on Earth, whose presence will outlast the adults. His threat joins the family story to ambitions for the planet.

Dragons' Seed

What Witchy calls the children when releasing them: "I break the egg! I turn you loose! Oh, my own Dragons' Seed!" See Chapter 16.

Drowning

Helen's curse specifies Crocker be "In else than water be you drownded!", fulfilled when he sinks into quicksand/bog rather than water.

Dulanty Armada

The two rafts, Île de France and Sea Bear, when operated jointly during the strip pit campaign.

Durance Frugal

Pandemonium’s comic variation on durance vile, an old expression for imprisonment or captivity. Burns uses the latter in “Epistle from Esopus to Maria.” Here the altered phrase appears in John’s domestic songs.

Sources: Merriam-Webster, “durance”.

E

Earl of the Ears

Peter's title for Charles after he returns from dealing with the blackbirders with "three pairs" of severed ears. See Chapter 9.

Earth Allergy

The sickness affecting Pucas on Earth. Early narration says the Earth-born children suffered it lightly and acquired immunity, but Peter later undergoes a violent fit of its passion; Witchy finally assures the children they will escape its fatal course.

Eigeas-Amhranai

The title applied to Pandemonium John as a high master of song. Irish éigeas denotes a poet or learned person and amhránaí a singer. The novel magnifies those ordinary lexical senses.

Eisimirce

Eisimirce means emigration in Irish; the novel glosses it as the Dispersal. It is not the name of the children’s naming rite.

Epiphany

The moment of revelation when a Puca realizes their true name. The naming ceremony in Chapter 16 is such an epiphany.

F

Faber Stronghold

One of the Five Kingdoms in the strip pits, raided by the Dulantys. The Fabers have "mean black eyes" and rumored to use children for bait. See Chapter 6.

Fear-ungadh-mac

A Puca term relating to "the Anointed," discussed by Henry Dulanty with the priest. Connected to the teaching that the Anointed will be born on the "meanest of worlds."

Fee Simple

An estate in land not limited to a specified class of heirs or a fixed duration. The deed applies the formula to the raft and its household accessories in exchange for whisky.

Feirin-De

Dorothy’s Puca name, plausibly “gift of God.” Irish féirín means a gift or keepsake. The exact compound is an interpretive reconstruction.

Fianaise

The Presence associated with Puca action and timing. Irish fianaise has senses of presence and witness.

Fiddler's Landing

One of the Five Kingdoms in the strip pits, home of Jack Fiddler who makes choc beer and corn whisky. The goat Catherine de Medici indicates its hidden location.

Five Kingdoms, The

The strip pit territories conquered or allied by the Dulantys: Stinkers, Stutgard Landing, Fiddler's Landing, Lanyard Landing, and Faber Stronghold.

Flying Snake

The briefly mentioned ship whose pirates are even bloodier than those of the Spanish Dancer in Helen’s account. Its passing appearance brings the number of ships named in that account to four.

Four Worlds, The

Earth is called one of the Four Worlds, but the four are not exhaustively mapped. Mercator, Astrobe, and Camiroi are named elsewhere; the novel does not say that they are the other three. Pandemonium John speaks of a dozen worlds.

Frank Dulanty

Henry's brother, Veronica's husband, father of Peter, Dorothy, John, and Bad John. Suffers Earth Sickness but uses alcohol as "a Specific against it." Publicly humiliates Crocker at Blind Ben's Bar. Dies in Chapter 15 when Marshall strikes him from behind while he's hanging Crocker. See Frank Dulanty.

Frolic of His Own

A phrase from the law of an employer’s responsibility for an employee’s acts: a frolic is a departure for personal purposes. Henry imagines Sammy using it to explain the car’s wreck.

Sources: “Frolic and detour,” Cornell Legal Information Institute.

Fulbert Fronsac

The town drunk who is also the lost Dauphin (or his son, or grandson). One of the few who can see Bad John, whom he calls "Ange-Jacques." Gives the children the Île de France and Catherine de Medici. See Fulbert Fronsac.

G

Gallant Band/Brood

Ironic descriptors for the Dulanty children, used in chapter titles: "High Purpose of the Gallant Band" (Ch. 3), "No Setting for the Gallant Brood" (Ch. 9).

Gargoyle

What the lawyer glimpses when he catches the Dulanty children in his peripheral vision, "a veritable gargoyle." The sideways glimpse reveals their true appearance.

Ghost Child

Bad John, who died as a baby but persists with the family. Translucent, invisible to most, unable to tell ghost stories ("he could only tell people stories"). See Bad John.

Gigantis Transfixus

The ship in Helen’s account whose mainmast is a living giant she claims to feed and water. The name suggests an impaled giant, but its Latin is irregular: gigantis is a genitive form, whereas transfixus is nominative. It is one of four ships named in the account.

Giver, The

Witchy's term for the divine being who gave the Puca their primitive ethic: "The only ethic that the Giver has given us yet."

Goblin

The non-human appearance of some Pucas: "potato-faced goblins, like real Pucas." Peter, Dorothy, John, and Bad John appear this way; Elizabeth, Charles, and Helen appear more human.

Grandmother Stories

Inherited Indigenous stories that preserve an expectation of Puca assistance. The men in the Lost Moon Bar recognize Henry’s kind through them.

Greek Fire

The historical name evokes Byzantine incendiary warfare. Helen’s version consists of burning paper sacks weighted with rocks and filled with injurious debris and dung. She claims that lockjaw will kill the victims by sundown.

Green Eyes

All Pucas have green eyes that glow in the dark, the novel's recurring motif. The final image: "Six little pair of goblin eyes glowing green in the dark."

Green River

The main waterway threading through the strip pits. The children spend their "first night at sea" moored here. In Chapter 16, it becomes their escape route to the Arkansas, the Mississippi, and the world.

H

Harvestman

A daddy longlegs. Harold Harvestman is said to be as spindly as one, making his surname a bodily joke. The passage names no real person behind his name.

Helen Dulanty / Lochrann

The youngest (6) and most intelligent, by her own assertion. Navigator of the Île de France. Master of Bagarthach (kills Crocker with early curse). Inventor of pirate ships and Greek Fire. See Helen.

Henry Dulanty

Witchy’s husband and father of Elizabeth, Charles, and Helen. Accused of Stutgard’s murder, he defeats Marshall in the jail fight. Marshall later shoots him three times and Crocker fires the fatal shot. Henry’s curse anticipates Marshall’s death by rifle.

Heroic Chantey

The Latin and English verse fragments appearing as section headers in Chapter 14, commemorating Henry's death. Ends with "Nil de Monstris Nisi Bonum."

Hive Ghost

The Puca term for Earth's collective human consciousness: "The Hive Ghost hovers over them invisible... We can handle Earth individuals as though they were furniture or stones, but we cannot handle the Hive Ghost."

Homo Pucalis

Henry’s learned self-designation, Homo Pucalis, or Puca Man. It parodies a species label; the novel gives no etymology meaning painted or disguised man.

Honkeroo

A grand jam session. The children and Pandemonium John hold one through afternoon and night, mixing Earth songs with Puca music. See Chapter 10.

Hounds

Crocker’s dogs are Luke, John, Barnabas, Timothy, Book, Bell, and Candle. Barnabas leaves first, then the other three hounds, then Book and Bell. Candle stays and passes under Marshall’s command.

I

Ifreann

Irish for hell. Peter names Earth and Ifreann as the origins of the figures in his story of Alice and Clarissa. The word is an Irish place-name in the children’s cosmology.

Île de France

The fishing raft Fulbert deeds to the children for whisky. Its imposing French name suits his royal pretensions; a particular ocean-liner reference is possible but unconfirmed.

Impressors

Those who take people by force into service. The narrator applies the term, alongside blackbirders, to the children Crocker hires to capture the Dulantys. See Blackbirders.

Ionuin

Bad John's Puca name, meaning "beloved." He departs Earth with Frank's spirit in Chapter 16: "He's gone with his father Frank."

J

Jack Fiddler

Whisky and choc-beer maker. Peter first holds him with the captured .22; Charles then takes the larger rifle. Fiddler escapes their attempted execution.

Jackal

Peter's Bagarthach warns Stutgard to "Beware of jackals bearing axes!" The warning is fulfilled when Crocker (the jackal) kills him with an axe. Frank calls Crocker's mob "jackals."

Job, Book of

Henry reads Job 30:28 in the Vulgate. The priest continues with selections from 30:29–31 in Douay-Rheims wording and recalls a professor’s nickname for Job, the Majestic Cry-Baby. The language of dragons and a mourning harp connects suffering with Henry’s own people and their art.

Sources: Job 30:28–31, Douay-Rheims.

John Dulanty / Dia-Ta-Caomh

Six years old and goblin-appearing. John finds Stutgard already murdered and takes Crocker’s axe. Helen, also six, is explicitly the youngest living child.

John Loose Screw

John Lewis Grew, a Quapaw who helps Henry escape. Provides information about Crocker's operations and introduces Henry to the Lost Moon Bar.

Judas Priest

A euphemistic oath in American English. Fulbert later uses the mock-French Judas Prêtre. It is one of several comic shifts among English, French, Irish, and Latin.

K

Kindred

Other Pucas. Frank says the children cannot use Bagarthach against Kindred while explaining the adults’ successful counterverse. The earlier inset story of a Puca husband killed through a verse complicates an absolute rule of immunity.

Kingdom of the Stinkers

The first of the Five Kingdoms conquered by the Dulantys. Home of Rex Remagen, Elwood Elgin, and Berny Bottleby. See Chapter 5.

Kobolds

German folkloric spirits associated with houses or underground places. The narrator says that Pucas more closely resemble kobolds than goblins. The underground associations suit their native habits and the mound refuge.

Sources: Merriam-Webster, “kobold”.

Koneion

Greek kōneion, hemlock. The lawyer invokes it in the tea scene; Helen returns the reference to Socrates with Peter’s silent help.

L

Laidir

Charles’s Puca name: Irish láidir, strong. He becomes the clan’s acknowledged patriarch at eight.

Lanyard Landing

One of the Five Kingdoms, home of Phoebe Jane Lanyard. Allied, not conquered: the Lanyards treat with the Dulantys peacefully.

Le Franc de Pompignan

Jean-Jacques Lefranc, marquis de Pompignan (1709–1784), French poet and member of the Académie française. Fulbert claims to have been his natural son in his youth and therefore to carry paper for an unexpected ode.

Sources: Académie française, Jean-Jacques Lefranc de Pompignan.

Little Egypt

A real name for southern Illinois. Witchy folds Cairo, Thebes, Shawneetown, Paducah, and an imaginary American Nile into her river geography. The Ohio actually joins the Mississippi at Cairo; Paducah is on the Ohio.

Sources: Alexander County, Illinois, history and geography.

Little Jack Wilson

A man summoned by Frank Dulanty's Bagarthach verse. He arrives without knowing why he walked to the hills, demonstrating the verse's power.

Little Sad Sam Jones

One of the blackbirders hired by Crocker, "big as a house" despite being called "Little." Armed with a shotgun. Charles captures him along with Baxter Bushyhead and Harold Harvestman.

Lochrann

Helen’s Puca name: Irish lóchrann, lamp or light. The name suits a navigator.

Lost Dauphin, The

Fulbert presents himself as the lost Dauphin, or his son or grandson, changing the claim as needed. The title recalls the lost French heir Louis XVII; the combination of a Dauphin and a raft also recalls the fraudulent king in Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, chapter 19. The resemblance goes beyond the shared title, but direct borrowing is unproved.

Sources: Twain, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, ch. 19.

Lost Haven

The overgrown town of the Dulantys’ six-week residence. Stutgard’s and Marshall’s schemes profit from departed, dead, and invented inhabitants.

Lost Moon Bar

The bar in which Henry meets recognition and practical aid from Indigenous allies during the hunt.

M

Mandrake Marshall

The primary antagonist: prosecutor, former football player, state legislator. Claims total recall from studying "Beautiful Letters." Sadistically murders Henry and Butterfield. Killed by Phoebe Jane's rifle, fulfilling Henry's Bagarthach. See Mandrake Marshall.

Metamorphosis

Helen's term for what happens when parents die: "After your parents die they turn into your ancestors. It's called metamorphosis."

Misu Mound

The burial mound where the children find affinity with the dead. Helen estimates thirty thousand occupants. She later trades a buffalo thighbone for a dead woman’s own thighbone, wanted for an artifact.

Moonrise

The signal for departure in Chapter 16: "We will cast off at moonrise, or at another rising a few seconds later." Charles keeps a moon-vigil from the highest tree.

N

Nautical Vocabulary

A binnacle houses a ship’s compass; a davit hoists boats or loads; abaft means toward the stern. A scuttle can be a hatch or a container. Helen’s story turns the binnacle into a rowboat and the davit into a water vessel. She misuses the terms for comic effect.

Sources: Merriam-Webster, “binnacle”; Merriam-Webster, “davit”; Merriam-Webster, “scuttle”; Merriam-Webster, “abaft”.

Neanderthal

Henry suggests that Pucas may have been the original Neanderthals.

Nil de Monstris Nisi Bonum

"Nothing about monsters except good" is the final line of the Heroic Chantey in Chapter 14. Parodies nil de mortuis nisi bonum ("nothing about the dead except good").

Noose

Frank Dulanty publicly places a noose around Crocker's neck at Blind Ben's Bar, "belling" him. Crocker keeps it on his bedpost where it "stares at him." Frank uses it to hang Crocker over the quicksand.

O

Oklahoma State ’63

Sammy Bluefield’s stated engineering credential. Read as a graduation date, it places the action in or after 1963. The institutional name is consistent with the university’s 1957 change from Oklahoma A&M.

Sources: Oklahoma State University, 1957 name change.

Outre Scene

The climactic scene in Puca drama where "all the bloody stuff is heaped together for the greater enjoyment and convenience of all. It is extravagant and outré. It is both tragic and comic in the tall burlesque of it." Chapter 15 is the novel's outre scene.

P

Pandemonium

John’s wheeled musical apparatus, including a resonator communicating with his home world. Milton’s Pandæmonium names the capital of Satan and his peers; the ordinary sense of uproar also suits the machine. Forty or fifty instruments describes the smaller seagoing apparatus proposed for Charles.

Sources: Milton, Paradise Lost 1.756–57.

Pandemonium John

A high Eigeas-Amhranai, master of song surpassing "Homer, Dante, and Shakespeare combined." Arrives "lean and crestfallen as a winter crow" with news from the parents. Leads the honkeroo. See Chapter 10.

Parting Admonition

Witchy's final charge to the children before death: the primitive ethic, "Be swift, and be sudden!" and the release: "I break the egg! I turn you loose!"

Peter Dulanty / Carraig

"The funniest-looking of them all except John." Best fabricator among the children. Suffers violent Earth Sickness episodes. Will design gears for the seagoing pandemonium. See Peter.

Phoebe Jane Lanyard

An Indian woman friendly to the Dulantys. Helps Witchy escape the asylum. Provisions the children. Shoots Marshall dead from ambush, fulfilling Henry's Bagarthach curse. "She wasn't free from bloodlust herself. She'd never liked Marshall at all."

Piger Tempus Edax Rerum

The chantey combines a word for slow or sluggish with Ovid’s tempus edax rerum, time the devourer of things. The printed masculine piger does not agree with neuter tempus; pigrum would. The novel’s wording is kept unaltered.

Sources: Ovid, Metamorphoses 15.234–36.

Piltdown Parallel

The fake Puca Man display recalls Piltdown, the fabricated human fossil. The novel does not name Piltdown, and Henry claims that his specimen is a deceased kinsman rather than an assemblage of mismatched bones.

Plague of Demons

Witchy’s description of how the children will seem to Earth people. A Plague of Demons is also Keith Laumer’s 1965 Berkley novel. The shared wording may echo it; no borrowing is shown.

Sources: SFWA, A Plague of Demons (1965).

Pokey

American slang for jail, used as the rhyme in Helen’s verse about getting her father out.

Poltergeister

German plural of Poltergeist, a noisy or disruptive ghost. In the lawyer’s waking dream the children seem to be poltergeists made visible because their speech and lip movements do not coincide.

Potato-Faced Goblins

The typical Puca appearance: "potato-faced goblins, like real Pucas." Peter, Dorothy, John, and Bad John look this way.

Primitive Ethic

The Puca moral code: "We live by a more primitive ethic... Be swift, and be sudden! That is our secret." Distinct from Earth's "peculiar and remarkable ethic" (Christianity).

Puca

The alien/fairy species to which the Dulantys belong. From Irish púca, a shape-shifting creature associated with harvest and trickery. Can appear human or goblin. Possess magical abilities through Bagarthach. See Puca Nature.

Puca Sleep Hold

A technique for rendering someone unconscious. Henry uses it on the jailer during his escape: "He throttled the jailer very gently and stretched him out to sleep on the floor."

Q

Quicksand

Crocker drowns in the bog after Frank loses his grip on the rope. Marshall rolls Frank in; Charles rolls Marshall in. The sequence fulfills Helen’s curse.

R

"Rang-Dang-Doo"

An Earth song performed during the honkeroo, alongside "Birmingham Jail Blues" and "Shotgun Boogie", one of the "great songs of Earth."

Reefs of Earth

The novel's central metaphor: Earth as a dangerous reef where the Dulantys are stranded. The Heroic Chantey laments: "Stranded Like a Fool, Dulanty— / On the Reefs of Earth—Untimely—"

Relief Checks

See "Checks, Relief." Stutgard's scheme to collect government payments for departed residents: "bogus relief checks might run around sixty thousand dollars a month."

Resonator

A component of Pandemonium John’s apparatus connected with the Puca home world. In the narrative John himself brings the parents’ news.

Rogers County

A county in northeastern Oklahoma where Lost Haven is located. Lafferty lived in nearby Tulsa.

S

Sagart

A Puca priest, the preferred recipient of confession, above ordinary Pucas or inhabitants of other worlds.

Salic Law

The French succession rule associated with excluding women and claims through a female line from the throne. Peter invokes it when he wants the raft deed written in a man’s hand. The joke transfers dynastic law to Crow Creek Township and an ordinary conveyance.

Salt of the Earth

Witchy’s charge borrows Matthew 5:13. It follows her advice to break the world and recalls her earlier story of a cousin who mutilates himself by taking Matthew 5:29–30 literally.

Sources: Matthew 5:13, 29–30, Douay-Rheims.

Sammy Bluefield

An Indian with a modified car: dozer blade, shot-proof glass, 139 mph. Helps Henry smash through a roadblock. Breaks his collarbone in the crash: "happens to me all the time."

Sea Bear

The Stinkers' raft, captured during the Battle of Port Stinker. Towed behind the Île de France.

"Shotgun Boogie"

An Earth song performed during the honkeroo, one of the "great songs of Earth."

Sideways Glimpse

How the Pucas' true appearance is revealed: "Look at them straight, and you'd hardly notice a thing. But look at them sideways accidentally sometimes... and they'd scare the liver out of you."

Silly Jimmy

A figure in Helen's ghost story who died when a train ran over him, "spattering him like tar." He supposedly still sits whittling by the tracks.

Spanish Dancer

A ship in Helen’s reported night adventure. She claims its pirates make her captain after recognizing her worth. Her story also names the Flying Snake, Vampire, and Gigantis Transfixus; the narration gives only her account of the encounter.

Specific Against

Frank treats alcohol as a Specific against Earth Sickness. The novel calls his later staggering illness, not drunkenness.

Spirits of Panther

Crocker’s strong liquor, also called Horse Whisky and the old cat-creature. The animal names belong to a family of slang expressions for potent or cheap alcohol. Fulbert’s White Lightning is moonshine.

Stinkers

The first group of strip pit children defeated by the Dulantys: Rex Remagen, Elwood Elgin, and Berny Bottleby. Their raft, the Sea Bear, is captured. See Chapter 5.

Stream of Thought

A phrase established in psychology by William James, whose Principles of Psychology gives it a chapter title. In the chantey it is joined to Earthen Sickness and Henry’s slowing decisions. The phrase echoes James; no specific debt is shown.

Sources: William James, The Principles of Psychology, ch. 9.

Strip Pits

"A dozen square miles of mountains turned inside out, threaded by canals, and with bottomless chasms." The remnants of surface coal mining, now the children's domain. See The Strip Pits.

Stutgard, Coalfactor

Claims to own Lost Haven. Murdered by Crocker with an axe before the children can kill him, fulfilling Peter's Bagarthach: "Beware of jackals bearing axes!" See Coalfactor Stutgard.

Stutgard Landing

One of the Five Kingdoms. After its conquest the children scratch the soil and sow salt. Judges 9:45 records Abimelech salting Sichem; the much-repeated Carthage story has a disputed later history. Neither city is named in this scene.

Sources: Judges 9:45; R. T. Ridley, “To Be Taken with a Pinch of Salt” (1986).

Substance and Accidentals

Scholastic terms invoked in the account of Peter’s Earth sickness. The substance is inhibited while accidentals are forced prematurely. The passage’s philosophical vocabulary describes distorted development. Compare Pandemonium’s account of Witchy confusing essence and accidence.

Sources: “Accident,” Catholic Encyclopedia.

T

"Take Me Back to Tulsa"

An Earth song considered one of its "great songs" along with "Birmingham Jail Blues," "Shotgun Boogie," and "Rang-Dang-Doo."

Talking Rifle

The instrument of Marshall's death, specified in Henry's Bagarthach: "A message from a talking rifle." Fulfilled when Phoebe Jane shoots Marshall.

Tide

A supernatural surge of power and timing experienced by Pucas, linked to the Fianaise or Presence. Frank relies on it during confrontations.

Time by the Forelock

The traditional image of opportunity as a figure with hair in front and baldness behind: it must be caught before it passes. The chantey deprives Time even of that forelock while Henry submits. Phaedrus’s “Tempus” provides an ancient version of the image.

Sources: Phaedrus, Fables 5.8, “Tempus”.

Timothy

One of Crocker's four hounds, younger but considered a "good one" along with Barnabas. Abandons Crocker during the final hunt.

Translucent

Bad John is visible yet translucent to those who can see him. He can enter locked rooms, but is not an ideal lookout: he cannot distinguish darkness.

True Puca

The authentic state of being a Puca: successful intuitive mastery of Puca arts. Charles may become one if he builds his pandemonium and learns the trade.

T-Town

Tulsa, Oklahoma. The lawyer Fulbert brings is from "T-Town." Henry had "places and people" there he intended to visit after escaping jail.

Two Art-Forms

Bagarthach verse and Aorach story are the principal verbal arts discussed in the novel. The narrator insists on literal happenings when explaining Puca art. Aorach ranges beyond ghost stories, and not every teller’s claim becomes fact.

U

Understanding, Gracious

A quality Witchy says was depleted "by the gallon" during the Pucas' failed attempt to coexist peacefully with Earth.

Under-Studding

A term of obscure menace in Peter's Bagarthach against Stutgard: "Beware the under-studding, Stut!" Possibly structural or metaphorical.

V

Vampire

A ship in Helen’s reported night adventure, whose crew receive blood with their rum. Its name is one of four ship names in that account.

How Close the Equivalents Are

Some pairings are close in meaning: Peter’s rock and Dorothy’s gift are the clearest. Others are looser. Charles’s Laidir means strong, which is not the historical meaning of Charles. The rite is called an Epiphany, a manifestation or opening out. Its dramatic purpose is the children’s recognition of themselves.

All sixteen chapter accounts and the reference sections have been revised against this text. A character’s report, an inset tale, an inference, and an interpretation are identified as such where the distinction affects the claim.

✦Quotations and Unusual Readings
